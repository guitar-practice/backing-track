How to get a song onto your phone

Tap Choose a song and pick any song or video in Files or Photos. That includes iCloud Drive, Downloads, Dropbox and Google Drive. mp3, m4a, wav, aiff and flac all work, and a video works too (the app uses its sound).

Songs you bought on iTunes: on a Mac, open the Music app and drag the song to your desktop, then AirDrop it to your phone and choose Save to Files. On a Windows PC, the songs are in your iTunes Media folder; copy one into iCloud Drive.

Apple Music and Spotify songs are copy-protected, so they can't be split, here or in any other app.

The first song downloads the splitter, an AI model of about 200 MB, so use Wi-Fi. It's saved on your phone after that. A typical song takes a few minutes to split; keep this page open while it works.

Split on your computer, play on your iPhone. Splitting needs more memory than an iPhone gives a web page, so it can close the page partway. A laptop has plenty. Each device keeps its own list of songs, so a song split on the laptop isn't on the phone until you send it over:

  1. On the laptop, open this page, go to Splitter and choose the song. When it's done, it's saved under Your songs.
  2. Pick it in Your songs and tap Send.
  3. Tap AirDrop or share, choose AirDrop, then pick your iPhone. (Or tap Save the file: it goes to the laptop's Downloads folder, and from there you can AirDrop it from Finder, drag it into iCloud Drive, or email it to yourself.)
  4. On the iPhone, AirDrop puts it in the Files app, in Downloads. If the phone asks what to do with it, choose Save to Files.
  5. Open this page on the iPhone, go to Splitter and tap Open a split song file. Choose Choose File (not Photo Library), then Browse, then Downloads (or iCloud Drive), and pick the song.

The song goes into the iPhone's own list, so you only send it once. A 4-minute song is a file of about 14 MB. The Home Screen icon and Safari keep separate lists, so open the file in whichever one you practice with.

Wear wired headphones if you can. Through the speaker, the mic records your loops along with you, and Bluetooth headphones drop to phone-call sound while the mic is on.

Looper settings
Plays the mic in your headphones while you record. Leave it off with the speaker, or it will howl.
On iPhone, everything plays much quieter through the speaker while the mic is on. This turns the mic off after each recording, so your loops play at full volume. Your phone may ask to use the mic again before the next one.
Timing fix:

Sound takes a moment to come out of the speaker and back into the mic, so each recording is moved back by this much to land on the beat. To measure it, unplug headphones, turn the volume up and stay quiet while it plays six clicks. Bluetooth headphones add roughly 150 to 250 ms, so if your loops come back late, raise it.

Loops stay until you clear them or close the page. They aren't saved.
